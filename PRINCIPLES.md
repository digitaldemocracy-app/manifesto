# The Seven Principles

The Manifesto states seven principles in a few lines each. This document explains them. It is longer because questions deserve answers, and shorter than a book because principles should fit in a head.

---

## 1. Everything is visible

**What it means.** Every proposal, every argument, every vote count, every rule of the platform, and every line of code that applies those rules is public, permanent, and verifiable by anyone, without an account.

**Why.** Trust in democracy has eroded because decisions are made in places we cannot see. A platform that asks people to trust it with their voice must earn that trust by having nothing to hide. Secrecy in process is where distortion lives.

**How.** All public events are recorded in an append-only ledger with cryptographic chaining: once written, an entry cannot be altered without breaking the chain, and anyone can check the chain. Moderated content is hidden from the default view, not deleted, and the moderation decision itself is recorded. Where the law requires that something be removed — a court order, a data-protection request — the removal is carried out, and the fact that it happened is recorded in the ledger. Nothing is erased in silence. The source code is open from day one, under a license that keeps it open.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean your vote is public (see Principle 2), and it does not mean personal data is exposed. Visibility applies to the process and the results, not to private information about people.

---

## 2. Your vote is yours

**What it means.** Your vote is secret, always. You can verify it was counted. You are free to tell anyone how you voted. But the system will never produce proof of how you voted — so no one can ever demand that proof from you.

**Why.** The secret ballot exists to protect people from pressure: an employer, a landlord, a family member, a local boss, a government. If a vote can be proven, it can be bought or coerced. If it can only be declared, coercion loses its grip: a claim without proof is worth nothing to the coercer. This design keeps both freedoms — to protect yourself and to stand in the open.

**How.** The technical name for this property is *receipt-freeness*, combined with *end-to-end verifiability*. Several cryptographic voting schemes offer it. Our commitment is to the property, not to any particular scheme; the chosen scheme will be public, reviewed, and replaceable.

**What it does not mean.** Delegations (Principle 4) and public positions taken in a debate are not secret: when you give your voice to someone, or argue for a side, that is a public act.

---

## 3. Each person exists only once, and counts exactly the same

**What it means.** One human being, one identity, one vote. No multiple accounts, no bots, no bought crowds, and no vote that weighs more than another.

**Why.** Online participation has been poisoned by fake accounts and amplification. A democracy where one person can speak as a thousand is not a democracy. Equal weight is the oldest democratic promise, and the one most often broken in practice.

**How.** Participation grows with verification, in levels. An email account lets you read and debate; a verified identity document lets you vote with full weight; further verification may add a trust marker. Every count is shown separately by verification level, so anyone can see how many verified people supported a result. Verification is a gate to voting, never a multiplier of votes.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean that experts, officials, or founders get more say. Expertise is shown (Principle 6); it is never weighted.

---

## 4. Every decision belongs to those it affects

**What it means.** Decisions are made at the level where they have effect: neighborhood, city, state, country, region, world. Only matters that genuinely belong to everyone are decided by everyone.

**Why.** Centralizing decisions far from the people they affect is one of the main causes of the distance we describe in the Manifesto. The opposite error, deciding everything globally, would replace one distance with another. The principle is sometimes called *subsidiarity*: the smallest competent level decides.

**How.** The platform is organized in geographic scopes. A proposal is born in a scope and can only be voted on by people who belong to it. Scope is fixed once a proposal leaves draft. Dual citizens and residents may belong to more than one scope. A global scope exists for what has no borders: the climate, the oceans, pandemics, the rules of the platform itself.

**What it does not mean.** It is not a world government. There is no global authority on the platform that can override a local decision. "Each people decides how far this goes" applies here most of all.

---

## 5. Debating is not fighting

**What it means.** Arguments are organized, not piled up. Each proposal has arguments for and against; each argument can be replied to once; the community rates arguments for how well they are reasoned, not for how much they are liked.

**Why.** Forums and social networks reward outrage and repetition. A democracy needs the opposite: the best idea rising even when it is unpopular at first. Structure is what makes that possible.

**How.** Arguments are classified by side. Replies are limited to one level to keep focus. A constructiveness score, computed from community review, orders what people see. Moderation is done by the community and recorded; content that breaks the rules is hidden, not erased.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean polite silence. Disagreement is the point. What is excluded is abuse, manipulation, and noise.

---

## 6. We choose the most capable to carry out what we decide together

**What it means.** Deciding what to do is equal for all. Choosing who plans and executes it is also done by all, but with full visibility of what each candidate has done and knows. Competence is transparent; it is not a privilege.

**Why.** Representative democracy's failure is not that we choose people to act for us. It is that we choose them blindly, and then cannot see what they do. Visible track records and verified expertise let people choose well, and let them check afterward.

**How.** Verified expertise appears as a badge tied to a field, after verification of credentials. The badge gives no extra vote weight. Those chosen to execute a decision have their mandate, their commitments, and their results recorded on the ledger, so that the next choice can be better informed than the last.

**What it does not mean.** It is not technocracy. No committee decides who is competent. People decide, in the open, with better information.

---

## 7. Some things are not put to a vote

**What it means.** The dignity, freedom, and fundamental rights of every person are not subject to majority decision on this platform.

**Why.** A majority can be wrong, and history shows that majorities have been used against minorities. A democracy that does not protect the person is only a counting machine. The limit on what can be voted is what makes the vote worth trusting.

**How.** The platform's rules exclude proposals that would strip rights from a person or a group. The Universal Declaration of Human Rights is the reference. Proposals of this kind are hidden from voting, and the decision to hide them is itself public and reviewable.

**What it does not mean.** It does not mean a list of forbidden topics. People can debate anything. What they cannot do is vote away someone else's rights.

---

## How these principles change

These principles are the platform's constitution. They can be amended, but only through the platform itself, by the people who use it, in the open, and with a higher bar than ordinary decisions. The first vote ever held on the platform will be on this Manifesto and these principles.
